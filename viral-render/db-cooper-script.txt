November 24, 1971. Thanksgiving Eve. At Portland International Airport, a quiet man in a dark business suit walks to the Northwest Orient Airlines counter. He pays cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle. The name he gives is Dan Cooper.

Nothing about him attracts attention. Witnesses later describe a man in his mid-forties, wearing a white shirt, black tie, and suit. He boards Northwest Orient Flight 305, a Boeing 727 carrying thirty-six passengers. He takes a seat near the rear of the cabin, orders a bourbon and soda, and waits.

A little after three in the afternoon, Cooper hands a flight attendant a note. At first she assumes it is a phone number and slips it into her purse. Cooper quietly tells her she should read it. The message says he has a bomb.

She sits beside him. Cooper opens an attaché case just enough for her to see wires and red-colored cylinders. Whether the device is real is never proven. What matters is what happens next. Cooper dictates his demands: two hundred thousand dollars in twenty-dollar bills, four parachutes, and a fuel truck waiting when the plane lands in Seattle.

The crew contacts authorities while the aircraft circles. On the ground, the FBI and local officials begin assembling the ransom. The serial numbers of the bills are recorded. Parachutes are obtained. When Flight 305 finally lands at Seattle-Tacoma Airport, Cooper gets exactly what he asked for.

He releases all thirty-six passengers and several crew members. But the story is only halfway over.

Cooper orders the remaining crew to take the aircraft south toward Mexico City. He specifies a low altitude, the landing gear down, the cabin unpressurized, and a slow speed. Those instructions matter because the Boeing 727 has something unusual: a rear staircase that can be lowered in flight.

Sometime after eight o'clock that night, flying through darkness and bad weather over the Pacific Northwest, the crew feels a sudden change in the aircraft. The rear staircase has been deployed.

When the plane later lands in Reno, Cooper is gone.

So are the ransom money and one of the parachutes.

The FBI gives the investigation a code name: NORJAK, short for Northwest Hijacking. Agents search the aircraft, interview witnesses, examine physical evidence, and chase leads across the country. Over the years, hundreds of people are considered. By the fifth anniversary, investigators have looked at more than eight hundred suspects.

But Cooper leaves behind very little. A black clip-on tie. Cigarette butts that would later be lost. A few fingerprints that cannot be conclusively tied to the hijacker. And the memories of flight attendants who sat only feet away from him.

Then, nine years later, the case changes.

In 1980, an eight-year-old boy named Brian Ingram is digging in the sand along the Columbia River when he finds three decaying bundles of twenty-dollar bills. The total is five thousand eight hundred dollars. The serial numbers match the Cooper ransom.

The discovery creates a new mystery instead of solving the old one. How did the money reach the riverbank? Did Cooper lose the bag during the jump? Was the money carried by water? Was it buried intentionally? Investigators could not establish a definitive explanation.

The biggest question remains the simplest one: did Cooper survive?

The conditions were brutal. It was dark. The weather was poor. Cooper was wearing business clothes and dress shoes. The parachute he used was not a modern steerable sport parachute. The terrain below was wooded and difficult. The FBI has long considered it possible that he died during the jump.

But no body was ever found. No parachute was conclusively recovered. And despite decades of tips, no suspect was definitively identified as the man from Flight 305.

One frequently discussed suspect was Richard Floyd McCoy, who carried out a similar hijacking and parachute escape less than five months later. The FBI investigated him, but eventually ruled him out, citing differences from the witness descriptions and other evidence.

Even Cooper's famous name contains a mistake. The hijacker called himself Dan Cooper. The initials D.B. came from early press reporting after investigators questioned a different man with those initials. The mistake stuck, and the mystery became known around the world as the D.B. Cooper case.

For forty-five years, the FBI continued reviewing tips and evidence. In July 2016, the Bureau announced that it was redirecting the investigative resources assigned to the case toward other priorities. The physical evidence was preserved, and the FBI said it would still consider specific new evidence connected to the parachutes or ransom money.

That leaves us with an extraordinary set of facts. A man bought a ticket under a false name. He calmly hijacked a commercial aircraft. He collected two hundred thousand dollars and four parachutes. He released the passengers. Then, somewhere between Seattle and Reno, he lowered the rear stairs of a Boeing 727 and disappeared into a stormy November night.

More than half a century later, we still do not know who he really was, whether he survived the jump, or what happened to most of the ransom.

And that uncertainty may be the reason the story refuses to die. D.B. Cooper is not famous because investigators proved what happened. He is famous because after thousands of hours of investigation, hundreds of suspects, and decades of searching, the final scene is still missing.

A man steps into the darkness.

And never comes back.
